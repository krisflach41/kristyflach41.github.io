## MODULE A — Be an Advisor to Combat Fintech (Files 1-4, pp 1-26)

### How the Mortgage Market Works
- **The Mortgage Cycle:** Borrower gets loan from lender → lender sells to aggregator → aggregator pools loans into Mortgage Backed Securities → MBS sold to investors → servicer collects payments and distributes to investors
- **Interest Rate Risk:** Lenders take on risk between origination and sale. They hedge by taking short positions in MBS
- **Rate Locks:** When a borrower locks a rate, the lender hedges that commitment by shorting MBS. If rates drop before closing, the lender's short position gains value, offsetting the below-market rate they're locked into
- **Servicer Economics:** Servicers pay ~1% upfront to acquire servicing rights, earn ~30 basis points annually from the servicing strip, and need roughly 3 years to break even
- **COVID-Era Mortgage Crisis (March 2020):** Massive rate drops caused servicing runoff, margin calls, and mark-to-market losses for servicers. The Fed's MBS purchases had unintended consequences for the servicing industry
- **Fed Funds Rate vs Mortgage Rates:** These are NOT the same thing. Fed Funds is an overnight rate; mortgage rates are long-term. They can move in opposite directions

### The Rule of 72
- Quick way to estimate how long it takes money to double: divide 72 by the interest rate
- Example: at 6% interest, money doubles in ~12 years (72 ÷ 6 = 12)

### Power of Amortization
- **Lump sum prepayments** dramatically reduce total interest and shorten the loan term
- **Bi-weekly payments** (26 half-payments per year = 13 full payments) accelerate payoff
- Example from course: applying cashflow savings to principal turned a 30-year mortgage into a 14-year, 2-month payoff with $237K increase in net worth

### Refinance — The Right Way to Evaluate
- **Correct method:** Compare interest savings, not payment savings. Principal is the borrower's own money — it's not a cost
- **The cost of waiting:** If a borrower waits for a lower rate, they're forgoing monthly savings in the meantime. The math often shows locking in now and refinancing later beats waiting
- **Do you skip a payment?** No. Mortgages are paid in arrears. Interim interest is paid at closing. This is a common misconception

### Payment Does Not Equal Cost
- Principal portion of a mortgage payment is NOT a cost — it's equity the borrower is building
- When comparing loans, exclude principal from the cost calculation
- A loan with a higher payment can actually be cheaper if more of each payment goes to principal

### Debt Consolidation
- Using home equity to pay off high-interest debt can reduce total monthly obligations even if the mortgage rate is higher than some of the debts
- The key is total monthly cashflow improvement
- Apply the monthly savings back to mortgage principal to accelerate payoff and build wealth faster

### Annual Percentage Rate (APR) — Why It's Deeply Flawed
- APR assumes the borrower keeps the loan for the full 30-year term — almost nobody does
- APR assumes zero inflation over 30 years
- APR treats interim interest (prepaid at closing) as a fee, which inflates it unfairly
- APR assumes a constant rate environment
- **The Loan Triangle:** Loan amount, rate, payment — know any two, solve for the third
- **Amount Financed and APR Triangle:** Related calculation framework
- **True breakeven vs APR comparison:** In the course example, the real breakeven was 18 years, not what APR suggested

## MODULE B — Understanding the Markets (Files 5-7, pp 27-43)

### Bonds
- **Treasury Bills:** Maturity of 1 year or less
- **Treasury Notes:** Maturity of 1-10 years
- **Treasury Bonds:** Maturity greater than 10 years
- **10-Year Treasury Note:** The most watched benchmark, but NOT directly tied to mortgage rates
- **Bond pricing:** Par (100), Premium (above 100), Discount (below 100)
- **Coupon rate:** The fixed interest rate paid to the bondholder
- **Inverse relationship:** Bond price and yield move in opposite directions (seesaw)

### Bond Concepts
- **Capital appreciation/loss:** Change in bond price between purchase and sale
- **Yield to Maturity (YTM):** Total return if held to maturity, accounting for price paid
- **Nominal, real, and effective interest rates:** Different ways to express return
- **Duration:** Measures bond price sensitivity to interest rate changes. Longer duration = more sensitivity
- **Callable bonds:** Issuer can redeem early. Investor gets higher coupon but limited upside
- **Austria 100-year bond:** Course example of extreme duration risk

### Mortgage Backed Securities (MBS)
- MBS are pools of mortgages packaged and sold to investors
- MBS price movements directly affect mortgage rates
- MBS have a coupon rate and a price component, just like bonds
- **MBS coupon is typically ~1% below the borrower's mortgage rate** (the spread covers lender, servicer, aggregator, securitizer)

### Convexity Buying Cycle
- Rate drops → refinances increase → fund duration shortens → fund managers buy 10-year Treasuries to extend duration → Treasury yields fall → MBS become more attractive by comparison → more MBS buying → mortgage rates drop further
- This self-reinforcing cycle can accelerate rate improvements

### What Drives Rates
- **Two main factors:** Credit quality/default risk and inflation
- **Inflation is the archenemy of bonds** — it erodes the buying power of the fixed return
- **Inflation defined:** Too many dollars chasing too few goods
- **Deflation defined:** The opposite — falling prices
- Duration amplifies inflation's impact on bond investors
- **Velocity of money:** How quickly money circulates through the economy. Debt initially creates velocity, then drags on it
- Declining velocity since 2000 = less inflation = downward pressure on rates

### Stocks vs Bonds
- Inverse relationship — they compete for the same investment dollar
- **Safe haven flows:** Geopolitical risk or economic fear → money flows from stocks to bonds → bond prices rise → rates improve
- **Market timing:**
  - Bond market: 8am-5pm ET
  - Stock market: 9:30am-4pm ET
  - Lender pricing typically issued: 10-10:30am ET
  - European market close: 11:30am ET
  - Specialists leave: 3:30pm ET (can cause volatility)
  - Fed bond purchases: usually 11am-2:30pm ET
- **Bull vs Bear markets:** 10% decline = correction, 20% decline = bear market

## MODULE C — Understanding Economic Reports (Files 8-11, pp 45-57)

### Why Economic Reports Matter
- They measure the health of the economy and directly impact markets and rates
- Reports are judged against market expectations — the surprise factor moves markets
- **Stronger than expected = good for stocks, bad for bonds (rates rise)**
- **Weaker than expected = bad for stocks, good for bonds (rates improve)**

### Inflation Reports
- **CPI (Consumer Price Index):** Fixed basket of goods, heavily weighted toward housing and medical costs
- **PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures):** The Fed's preferred measure. Allows substitutions (e.g., if steak gets expensive, assumes consumers switch to chicken). Tends to underestimate real inflation
- **PCE typically runs softer than CPI** — yet the Fed prefers it
- **Headline vs Core inflation:** Core strips out food and energy (volatile items). Fed focuses on Core
- **Inflation is calculated on a rolling 12-month basis:** You can predict the trend by comparing what month is rolling off vs what new month replaces it
- **PPI (Producer Price Index):** Wholesale/producer-level inflation. Leading indicator but rarely moves markets on its own

### Employment Reports
- **ADP Report:** Private sector only, serves as a preview of the official jobs report
- **BLS Jobs Report:** Comprehensive, released first Friday of each month
  - **Establishment Survey:** Surveys businesses, produces the headline jobs number
  - **Household Survey:** Phone calls to individuals, produces the unemployment rate
- **U3 vs U6 unemployment:** U6 includes part-time workers wanting full-time and discouraged workers who stopped looking. U6 is the better measure of real unemployment
- **Average Weekly Earnings** is a better indicator of wage-driven inflation than Average Hourly Earnings
- **Initial Jobless Claims:** Released weekly on Thursday. Continuing Claims tracks ongoing unemployment

### Housing Reports
- **Existing Home Sales (NAR):** ~85% of the market. Reported monthly
- **Pending Home Sales:** Leading indicator — contracts signed but not yet closed
- **New Home Sales (Census Bureau):** ~15% of the market
- **Median home price is NOT appreciation** — it's simply the middle-priced home that sold in a given period
- **Case-Shiller Index:** Gold standard for home price measurement, but has a 2-month lag
- **FHFA HPI:** Conforming loans only, tends to show higher appreciation than Case-Shiller
- **CoreLogic HPI:** Includes a forecast component
- **Housing Starts and Building Permits:** Future supply indicators. Single-family starts are the most important component
- **NAHB Housing Market Index:** Builder confidence survey. Above 50 = expansion

### Other Economic Reports
- **Media often gets it wrong:** Ignores revisions to prior months, sensationalizes headlines
- **Mortgage Applications (MBA):** Weekly, released Wednesday. Purchase vs refi mix matters. The rate component can be misleading
- **Cass Freight Index:** Early recession indicator — measures freight shipment volume
- **GDP:** Quarterly, three readings per quarter (advance, preliminary, final). Two consecutive negative quarters = technical recession, but we don't know we're in one until after
- **Output Gap:** Real GDP vs potential GDP. Negative gap = excess capacity = less inflation pressure
- **Treasury Bond Auctions:** 10-year, 20-year, and 30-year are most important for mortgage markets. Watch the bid-to-cover ratio (demand indicator) and foreign participation levels

## MODULE D — The Federal Reserve and Recession Indicators (Files 12-17, pp 59-82)

### The Need for Central Banking
- **Credit:** Brings a future purchase to today. Can create velocity of money initially, then becomes a drag
- **Liquidity:** How easily an asset converts to cash
- **Fractional reserves:** Banks lend out deposits, keeping ~10% in reserves (post-Dodd-Frank)
- Banks borrow from each other at the Fed Funds Rate, or from the Fed's Discount Window
- Pre-2008 leverage ratios were ~50:1; post-Dodd-Frank reduced to ~10:1

### History of Central Banking
- **First Bank of the US (1791-1811):** Alexander Hamilton's creation
- **Second Bank of the US (1816-1836):** Andrew Jackson opposed it
- **Panic of 1907:** Bank runs nearly collapsed the system. J.P. Morgan personally intervened to provide liquidity
- **Jekyll Island (1910):** Secret meeting of bankers and politicians laid the foundation for the Federal Reserve
- **Federal Reserve created in 1913** in response to the Panic of 1907

### How the Fed Works
- **Primary function:** Ensure smooth market function through injection of liquidity
- **Repurchase Agreements (Repos):** Short-term borrowing tool — key mechanism for providing liquidity
- **Fiscal Policy** (government — taxes and spending) vs **Monetary Policy** (Fed — interest rates and money supply)
- **Fed Funds Rate:** Lowering = easing (stimulative), Raising = tightening (restrictive)
- **Quantitative Easing (QE):** Fed buys MBS and Treasuries → pushes prices up, yields/rates down
- **Quantitative Tightening (QT):** Fed sells or lets holdings run off → prices fall, yields/rates rise

### Federal Reserve Structure
- **Federal Reserve Act:** Dual mandate = maximum employment + price stability
- **12 Federal Reserve Districts**
- **FOMC (Federal Open Market Committee):** 19 members, 12 vote at any given meeting
- **Board of Governors:** 7 members, 14-year terms, appointed by the President
- **Dovish:** Favors lower rates, focuses on employment
- **Hawkish:** Favors higher rates, focuses on controlling inflation

### Fed Communications
- **FOMC Statements:** 6 features — economic developments assessment, goals reiteration, decision announcement, forward guidance, decision process explanation, voting record
- **Fed Dot Plot:** Anonymous forecast from each member showing where they think the Fed Funds Rate will be in the future
- **Fed Minutes:** Released 3 weeks after each meeting. Can move markets. Mortgage Bonds often react negatively on release days due to inflationary concerns. Can be dated if the Fed has spoken publicly since the meeting

### Money Printing and Inflation
- **Fed Funds Rate and mortgage rates are NOT the same** — can move in opposite directions
- When Fed Funds was at zero (2009-2016), mortgage rates were still 3%-5%
- A Fed rate cut can cause mortgage rates to RISE if the cut is interpreted as inflationary
- Educate clients ahead of expected Fed actions to prevent unrealistic expectations
- **The Wealth Effect:** Rising home values and stock prices make people feel wealthier → they spend more → economic activity increases
- **TINA ("There Is No Alternative"):** The Fed intentionally drives rates low to discourage saving, pushing money into stocks. Ben Bernanke was transparent about this strategy
- **Why Treasuries exist:** Government borrows by selling Treasuries (full faith and credit of the US, "risk free" at maturity) rather than printing money
- **Printing money devalues currency and creates inflation.** Germany 1920s hyperinflation: bread went from 63 marks (1918) to 201 billion marks (November 1923). Wiped out everyone's savings instantly
- **Hyperinflation** = inflation at 50%+ per year

### Recession Indicators
- Forecasting a recession = forecasting a refinance opportunity
- If recession is on the horizon: avoid paying upfront fees (points, single premium MI), consider higher rate in exchange for closing cost credits

#### The Unemployment Rate — Most Reliable Indicator (100% Accurate)
- Counter-intuitive: recessions do NOT happen when unemployment is high
- Recessions occur after unemployment hits its LOWEST level and begins to tick higher
- Mechanism: businesses hire to meet demand → unemployment drops → expansion slows → businesses cut headcount → newly unemployed spend less → other businesses feel slowdown → cycle perpetuates into recession
- Barry Habib used these indicators to forecast the 2020 recession before COVID hit

#### Increasing Corporate Debt
- Rising corporate debt often coincides with recessions
- Added debt service makes businesses more vulnerable to downturns → faster layoffs when business declines

#### Inverted Yield Curve
- Normal: longer maturities yield MORE than shorter ones
- Inverted: longer maturities yield LESS — investors expect future price declines (recession)
- Key measure: 10-year Treasury yield minus 2-year Treasury yield. Negative = inversion
- Recessions historically follow yield curve inversions

#### Synchronized Recessions
- Average ~50% of global economies in recession simultaneously
- Countries not in recession can help pull others out
- COVID 2020: ~93% of global economies entered recession — extreme synchronicity = longer, harsher recession

#### World Trade
- Data from the Dutch — very reliable recession indicator
- Recessions occur when world trade breaks below zero and trends lower (not when it's merely flat)

#### Using Your Crystal Ball
- One indicator alone could signal a recession, but a CONFLUENCE of indicators gives a much stronger forecast

### Choosing the Best Loan
- **No cookie-cutter answer** — each client has unique circumstances: savings goals, cashflow, time in home
- Most loan originators just quote the lowest rate and treat the mortgage as a commodity — the lowest rate may NOT be the best option
- **Principal is NOT a cost** — it's the customer's equity. Exclude it when comparing loans
- **True cost of a loan:** Closing costs + interest paid + cost of points (if any) + mortgage insurance (if any)
- Must consider how long the client will hold the loan — this changes which option wins
- **Example:** Loan A costs $5,000 more total at year 6, but builds $7,500 more in equity → Loan A is actually $2,500 better
- **Advisor Consultation:** Ask about family growth plans, future college needs, retirement timeline, career changes — these impact how long they'll hold the loan and when refinance opportunities arise
- **Plan loans two at a time** — think of the current loan AND the next loan as puzzle pieces. This is what turns you from a rate quoter into a debt manager — their Mortgage Advisor
- **CMA advantage:** Forecast future rate environments, utilize debt effectively through consolidation, accelerate principal payments with cashflow savings

## MODULE E — Technical Analysis (Files 18-21, pp 83-109)

### Two Methodologies: Eastern and Western
- **Eastern Technical Analysis:** Focuses on individual candles or candle series — identifies patterns and reversal signals
- **Western Technical Analysis:** Focuses on target levels that act as support or resistance
- Using both together = best chance of correctly identifying trends
- Western signals contextualize Eastern candle patterns

### WESTERN TECHNICAL SIGNALS

#### Support and Resistance Levels
- **Support (floor):** Price level where a downtrend pauses due to concentration of buying interest
- **Resistance (ceiling):** Price level where an uptrend pauses due to concentration of selling interest
- **Trading range** = the space between support and resistance
- These are self-fulfilling prophecies: professional traders set buy/sell triggers at the same levels, creating the moves they predict

#### Gap Higher / Gap Lower
- A security doesn't have to open where it closed the prior day
- After-hours trading or premarket news shifts the opening price
- **Gap Higher** = strong premarket buying; **Gap Lower** = strong premarket selling

#### Moving Averages (MA)
- Trend-following (lagging) indicators that smooth out short-term noise
- **Key MAs:** 25-day, 50-day, 100-day, 200-day
- Longer timeframe MAs = stronger support/resistance
- **200-day MA** is the most significant — a convincing break above or below = "sea change" signal
- Each trading day = 1/200th influence on the 200-day line
- Temporary breaks can be "head fakes" — need convincing breakout to confirm
- Shorter-term MAs (25-day) still act as support/resistance but are broken more frequently

#### Rule of Polarity
- Broken support becomes future resistance
- Broken resistance becomes future support

#### Leash Effect
- When price strays too far above or below its 25-day MA, it tends to snap back (like a dog on a leash)
- Signals that a reversal may be imminent

#### Golden Cross (Bullish Signal)
- Shorter-term MA crosses ABOVE longer-term MA
- Most powerful: 50-day MA crosses above 200-day MA
- Rallies tend to follow

#### Death Cross (Bearish Signal)
- Shorter-term MA crosses BELOW longer-term MA
- Most telling: 50-day MA crosses below 200-day MA
- Sharp declines tend to follow

#### Fibonacci Retracement Levels
- Based on the Golden Ratio (1.618:1) — found in nature, human body, galaxies
- **Levels:** 0%, 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, 76.4%, 100%
- Used to identify interim support/resistance within a trend
- Must choose the right range (recent trend high/low, NOT all-time extremes)
- Back-test accuracy once lines are drawn
- **Example:** Range 20-80 (difference = 60), 23.6% retracement = support at 65.84

#### Stochastic Indicator
- Measures momentum, direction, and overbought/oversold conditions
- Uses 21-day period (approximates trading days in a month)
- **%K formula:** (Most Recent Close − Lowest Low) / (Highest High − Lowest Low) × 100
- **%D line:** 3-day moving average of %K
- **Overbought:** Above 80%; **Oversold:** Below 20%
- Being overbought/oversold alone is NOT a buy/sell signal — can remain there extended periods

#### Stochastic Crossovers
- **Positive Crossover:** %K crosses above %D from oversold position → bullish (buy signal)
- **Negative Crossover:** %K crosses below %D from overbought position → bearish (sell signal)
- **Fast Stochastic:** Earlier signal but less reliable (more false signals)
- **Slow Stochastic:** Uses %D from Fast as %K in Slow, then calculates new %D as 3-day MA → more reliable, should be respected

### EASTERN TECHNICAL SIGNALS

#### Windows (Eastern version of Gaps)
- **Falling Window:** Gap between prior session's low and next session's high → top of window becomes resistance
- **Rising Window:** Gap between prior session's high and next session's low → bottom of window becomes support

#### Double Top (Bearish Reversal)
- Security hits a high → declines → rallies back to the same high → sellers from the first peak exit at breakeven → price declines again
- Human nature drives the pattern — wanting to exit a losing trade at breakeven
- Once formed, the double top level becomes formidable resistance

#### Bullish Engulfing (2-day, after downtrend → reversal higher)
- Day 1: small red body; Day 2: long green body that completely engulfs Day 1's red body
- Green candle opens below prior red close, closes above prior red open

#### Bearish Engulfing (2-day, after uptrend → reversal lower)
- Day 1: small green body; Day 2: long red body that engulfs Day 1's green body
- The lower Day 2 goes, the more significant the reversal

#### Bullish Hammer (1-day, at bottom of downtrend)
- Short body + long lower wick (at least 2× body) + little/no upper wick
- Can be red or green. Bears lost conviction intraday; Bulls took control

#### Bearish Hanging Man (1-day, at top of uptrend)
- Same shape as Hammer but after an uptrend
- Bears made their first attempt at driving prices lower; buyers were "hung out to dry"

#### Bullish Inverted Hammer (1-day, at bottom of downtrend)
- Small body + long upper wick (at least 2× body) + little/no lower wick
- Bulls looking to drive prices up; emotion is mirror opposite of Hanging Man

#### Bearish Shooting Star (1-day, at top of uptrend)
- Same shape as Inverted Hammer but after an uptrend
- Bearish reversal signal

#### Doji / Doji Star (1-day, signals indecision)
- Open and close are virtually the same; small upper and lower wicks
- Neither bulls nor bears have conviction
- Does NOT predict direction — predicts VOLATILITY
- Next candle after Doji typically dictates direction

#### Bullish Morning Star (3-day, after downtrend → reversal higher)
- Day 1: long red candle; Day 2: short-bodied candle (bears losing conviction); Day 3: long green candle (bulls take control)

#### Bearish Evening Star (3-day, after uptrend → reversal lower)
- Day 1: long green candle; Day 2: short-bodied candle (bulls losing conviction); Day 3: long red candle (bears take control)

### Signal Confluence
- No single signal is 100% accurate — they provide an edge, not certainty
- **The greater the confluence of signals agreeing, the greater the odds of accuracy**
- A Stochastic crossover confirmed by a candlestick pattern at a Fibonacci level near a moving average = high-confidence signal

### RATE LOCK DECISIONS

#### Core Principle
- The right strategy with a competitive rate beats the lowest rate with the wrong strategy every time
- Rate IS important — you cannot unlock a loan
- Executing at the right time is what matters most

#### Following Live MBS Pricing
- Watch the UMBS 30YR Bond Card — three components: Bond Price, Day Change, and Change from Lender Pricing Windows
- **Day Change** = movement from prior day's close (good to know but NOT the key metric)
- **Change from pricing window** = how much MBS has moved since YOUR LENDER issued pricing — this is what matters for re-price risk

#### The Day Change Trap
- Day Change can show positive while your actual pricing has worsened
- Example: Day Change +6bp, but MBS is actually DOWN 16bp from when lender priced at 10:00 AM → likely intraday re-price for the worse
- **Rule of thumb:** Lenders consider re-pricing when MBS moves ~12bp from when they issued pricing (varies by lender)

#### Don't Be Single-Minded on Lock Decisions
- MBS pricing direction matters, but must be combined with technical analysis
- A Bullish Hammer or drop to key support could mean prices are near a bottom despite worsening
- Avoid locking just before a significant improvement
- **Example from course:** Sharp price drop on June 5th formed a Bullish Hammer. Price then tested dual support (50-day + 25-day MA) for ~5 days. Patience was rewarded as market rallied, pricing improved significantly, rates moved lower

#### News and Events Can Override Technicals
- Technical analysis provides a distinct edge, not certainty
- Major news/events may override even the most reliable technical signals

#### Which MBS Coupon to Follow
- MBS coupons are offered in half-percent increments
- Borrower rate is typically ~1% above the MBS coupon (to cover lender, servicer, aggregator, securitizer)
- 3% mortgage rate environment ≈ 2% MBS coupon; 3.5% environment ≈ 2.5% coupon
- When Fed is buying MBS, check the NY Fed's operations (newyorkfed.org) to see which coupon they're targeting
- If Fed shifts buying focus (e.g., from 2.5% to 2% coupon), shift your monitoring accordingly

## KEY THEMES — What Makes a CMA Different

### 1. Advisor, Not Order Taker
A CMA doesn't just quote rates. They understand the forces that move rates, can forecast where rates are heading, and build the right loan strategy for each client's unique situation — planning loans two at a time and managing debt holistically.

### 2. Payment Does Not Equal Cost
The single most important concept a CMA can teach borrowers. Principal is equity, not expense. Comparing loans correctly requires excluding principal and focusing on true costs relative to the time the client will hold the loan.

### 3. Confluence Is Everything
Whether it's recession indicators, technical signals, or rate lock timing — no single data point tells the whole story. The power is in multiple signals confirming the same direction. This applies to markets, to economic forecasting, and to advising clients.

### 4. Educate the Client
Most consumers don't understand that Fed Funds ≠ mortgage rates, that principal isn't a cost, that APR is deeply flawed, or that the lowest rate isn't always the best deal. The CMA's job is to educate and advise, not just transact.

### 5. The Right Strategy Beats the Lowest Rate
Timing, loan structure, debt consolidation, principal acceleration, and planning for the next loan — these create more value than chasing the absolute lowest rate. A CMA with a competitive rate and the right strategy will outperform a commodity lender with the lowest rate every time.

## MBS HIGHWAY ADVISORY TOOLS — Concepts & Client Value

### Buy vs Rent Report
**Purpose:** Shows the long-term financial impact of buying versus renting over a selectable review period (default 9 years).

**Net Gain by Buying a Home** — the core output, combining six components:
1. **Appreciation Gain:** Difference between home's future value and current value. Can use forecast, historical, or custom appreciation rates
2. **Amortization Gain:** Equity accumulated from principal payments. Can model additional monthly payments, lump sums, and recasting (re-amortizing after lump sum paydown)
3. **Cashflow Difference:** Total rent cost minus total buying cost. Shows short-term cash impact. Even when buying costs more monthly, long-term wealth-building through appreciation and equity typically produces significant net gain
4. **Purchase Closing Costs:** Closing costs, credits, prepaids and escrows
5. **Tax Benefit Over Renting:** Based on filing status and tax rate — captures mortgage interest deduction advantage
6. **Cost to Sell Estimate:** Percentage of future home value (default 6%) for agent fees

**Client value:** Demonstrates that even when buying costs more month-to-month, appreciation gain + amortization gain typically far exceed the cashflow difference. In the example: buying cost $5,949 more over 9 years, but produced $61,689 in amortization gain and $211,198 in appreciation gain.

### Affordability Calculator
**Purpose:** Shows what a client can afford and how the numbers break down.

**Key outputs:**
- **Affordable home price** with proposed DTI ratio, adjustable via slider
- **Mortgage Payment Breakdown:** How much goes to principal & interest vs taxes & insurance
- **Budget Ranges:** How much home the client can afford at different DTI thresholds (e.g., below 30% vs above 30%)
- **Closing Costs Estimate:** Total funds needed (down payment + closing costs)
- **DTI Tolerance:** Default 43%, some loans allow up to 55%. Takes into account monthly obligations, insurance, taxes, and other expenses

### Amortization Calculator
**Purpose:** Shows how a loan changes over time — the shift from interest-heavy to principal-heavy payments.

**Key concepts:**
- **Summary:** Loan Amount, APR, total payments, annual rate, monthly P&I, total paid, total interest
- **How payments change over time:** Early in the loan, most of each payment goes to interest. As the loan progresses, more goes to principal, accelerating balance reduction
- **Full amortization schedule:** Yearly and monthly views showing how each payment splits between principal and interest, plus remaining balance — demonstrates long-term impact of payments or additional contributions

### Appreciation Calculator
**Purpose:** Projects home value growth, appreciation dollars, and return on investment over 1-15 years.

**Key outputs:**
- **Estimated Home Value:** Starting value + total appreciation gained
- **Appreciation:** Dollar amount change over the review period, plus average annual change
- **Return on Investment:** Total appreciation after closing costs divided by down payment. Example: ($447,133 − $40,000) ÷ $160,000 × 100 = 254% ROI
- **Comparison Chart:** Compares future home values for up to two appreciation rate scenarios

### ARM vs Fixed Calculator
**Purpose:** Compares adjustable-rate and fixed-rate mortgages side by side across two scenarios.

**Two scenarios modeled:**
1. **Monthly savings kept as cash:** Shows cashflow savings, principal difference, total savings, and break-even timeline (when ARM begins costing more than fixed)
2. **Monthly savings applied to principal:** Shows accelerated paydown effect — lower remaining balance over time, greater total savings, extended break-even

**Key sections:**
- **Loan Comparison Before Adjustment:** Baseline comparison during ARM's initial fixed period
- **Amortization & Principal Paid:** How each loan amortizes, filtered by scenario
- **Loan Comparison During Adjustment:** Impact once ARM hits first rate adjustment
- **Summary at adjustment point:** Remaining balance, updated rate, new payment for each option

### Bid Over Asking Price Report
**Purpose:** Shows clients how long it takes to recoup costs of bidding above asking price through appreciation.

**Key outputs:**
- **Break-Even Timeline:** Months until home value exceeds total bid amount
- **5-Year Projected Value:** Projected home value vs asking price, bid over ask, and total bid
- **5-Year Cumulative Gain:** Total appreciation in dollars and percentage, with 1-year forecast, 5-year forecast, and historical average benchmarks
- **Visual chart:** Red = home value below total bid; Green = home value exceeds bid and equity gained

### Blended Rate Calculator
**Purpose:** Combines multiple debts into a single weighted average rate, giving a clear picture of overall borrowing cost.

**Key outputs:**
- **Total amount:** Combined balance of all debts
- **Blended rate:** Weighted average interest rate — debts with larger balances influence it more
- **Total monthly payment:** Sum of all minimum payments
- Supports up to 15 debts with descriptive labels, balances, rates, and monthly payments

**Client value:** Opens the door to debt consolidation conversations by showing the true combined cost of carrying multiple debts.

### Cost of Waiting Report
**Purpose:** Quantifies the financial cost of delaying a home purchase.

**Core equation:** Appreciation Gain + Cumulative monthly payment difference (buying now vs waiting) − Cost of Refinance = Benefit of Buying Now

**Key outputs:**
- **Net cost of waiting** for the selected duration
- **Appreciation Gain** of the property over time
- **Payment Difference chart:** Monthly payment now vs what it would be if purchased later at a higher price/rate
- **Full property and loan details breakdown**

### Debt Consolidation / Move-Up Purchase Report
**Purpose:** Shows the financial impact of consolidating debts into a new mortgage or move-up purchase.

**Key outputs:**
- **Front ratio** (housing expenses ÷ gross monthly income) and **back ratio** (total monthly debts ÷ gross monthly income) with max thresholds and over/under amounts
- **Available Cash In-Hand, LTV, and applicable debts** — client selects which debts to include in the new loan
- **Monthly deficit or savings** from consolidation
- **Overall savings through debt consolidation**
- **Payment comparison:** Existing mortgage + debts vs proposed consolidated loan
- **Full loan details breakdown:** All loans, debts, obligations, and equity in existing vs proposed scenarios

### Equity Gained Report
**Purpose:** Projects potential home equity over up to 15 years using one or two appreciation models.

**Key outputs:**
- **Appreciation Gained + Amortization Gained = Total Equity Gained** — shows how both market growth and loan repayment contribute
- **Equity visualization graph:** Home value growth lines (by appreciation rate) vs remaining loan balance over time, with house icons (projected value) and dollar icons (loan balance) at milestone years

### Investment Property Report
**Purpose:** Analyzes rental property cash flow, returns, and long-term investment performance.

**Key outputs:**
- **Monthly cash flow (Year 1)** and **cumulative cash flow** through ownership period (up to 10 years)
- **Cumulative Cash on Cash Return:** Annual cashflow as a percentage of initial outlays (down payment, closing costs, renovation costs)
- **Estimated Annual Cash Flow:** Rental income, expenses (P&I, taxes, insurance, maintenance, HOA, property management), total cash flow, and cash on cash return per year
- **Total Return on Investment:** Gross revenue (rental income + appreciation) minus all expenses = total profit, expressed as percentage of amount invested

### Reinvestment Calculator
**Purpose:** Illustrates how reinvested funds grow over time through compound interest.

**Key outputs:**
- **Ending Balance:** Projected total investment value at end of selected timeframe
- **Interest Gained:** Total amount earned from interest
- **Contributions:** Total invested (lump sum + periodic payments)
- **Visual:** Solid line (contributions) vs dashed line (total balance with compounding) — dashed line grows faster over time, demonstrating the power of compound returns

### Refi Risk of Waiting Report
**Purpose:** Shows the true cost of delaying a refinance while hoping for a better rate.

**Key outputs:**
- **Cost of Delaying:** (Existing Payment − Refinance Now Payment) × Months Waiting
- **Break-Even Period:** Savings Lost by Delaying ÷ (Refinance Now Payment − Future Refinance Payment)
- **Three loan scenarios compared:** Existing loan (no refi), Refinance Now, Refinance at hypothetical future rate
- **Client value:** Shows not just monthly savings but how long it takes to recoup the cost of waiting — and whether immediate cashflow improvement outweighs waiting for a potentially lower rate

### Seller Contribution Report
**Purpose:** Compares the cost and benefit of different seller contribution strategies: price reductions, temporary buydowns, permanent buydowns, and seller-paid closing costs.

**Key outputs:**
- **My Property chart:** Details of each contribution scenario with highlightable cells
- **Seller Costs / Buyer Benefits:** Clear presentation for each scenario
- **Lowest Payment chart:** Which option costs the buyer least over the selected timeframe (cumulative P&I, taxes, insurance, HOA). Scenarios color-coded on graph
- **Summary breakdown:** Seller costs, closing costs, interest, principal, mortgage insurance per scenario. Green = benefit vs no contribution; Red = loss vs no contribution

## MBS PRICING: CORE FRAMEWORK — How Mortgage Rates Actually Work

This is foundational knowledge that underpins everything a CMA does. This section must be preserved in full.

### The Central Truth: Rates Are a Price Sheet, Not a Menu That Appears and Disappears

In agency mortgage lending, most retail note rates are typically **available on most days**. What changes is the **price** of each rate, not usually the existence of the rate itself. A lender can generally price a menu of note rates across a ladder, then assign each one a premium, par value, or discount based on current secondary-market execution. Fannie Mae's whole-loan pricing framework explicitly supports this by allowing lenders to work within ranges of pass-through rates rather than needing one exact borrower note rate.

**The rate sheet is not primarily deciding what rates exist. It is deciding what each rate costs today.**

### Borrower Note Rate ≠ MBS Pass-Through Rate

A borrower's note rate is not the same thing as the MBS pass-through rate. In agency execution, the pass-through rate is derived from the borrower's note rate after subtracting servicing compensation and the guaranty fee. Fannie Mae states this directly: the pass-through rate is calculated by subtracting the servicing fee and guaranty fee from the borrower interest rate. Freddie Mac pass-through securities function similarly as pooled mortgage cash-flow instruments guaranteed by Freddie Mac.

### Why Almost Every Rate Exists Every Day

Retail mortgage pricing is built from a **rate ladder**. The lender does not usually decide whether 6.125%, 6.250%, 6.375%, or 6.500% "exist." Those note rates can generally all be quoted. The market determines the **economic value** of each option. One rate may require discount points, another may be near par, and another may generate enough premium to provide lender credits. CFPB guidance reflects this exact tradeoff: points buy the rate down; lender credits increase the rate in exchange for lower upfront cost.

On the secondary-market side, lenders are normally pricing toward one or more agency coupon executions. Fannie Mae's structure allows delivery across a range of five consecutive pass-through rates in 0.125% increments under the same mandatory commitment, and pricing can even be browsed in finer 0.025% increments. That is one of the main structural reasons lenders can usually still quote many borrower note rates on the same day.

### What Actually Changes Day to Day

What changes day to day is the **price/yield relationship** between the lender's pipeline and the secondary market. If MBS prices fall, the lender usually worsens pricing for the same retail note rates. If MBS prices rise, the lender usually improves pricing for those same rates. In practical terms, the same 30-year fixed note rate may still be available, but the cost may move from a lender credit to par, or from par to borrower-paid discount points.

This is why rate sheets are really **price sheets**. The retail note rate is only one coordinate. The more important variable is the price attached to that rate for that lock term, loan type, and borrower profile. Secondary execution moves first; retail pricing follows. Fannie Mae describes its whole-loan pricing as live pricing that generally moves with the MBS market.

### The 8 Main Drivers of MBS Pricing

#### 1. Treasury Market and Base Interest-Rate Moves
Agency MBS trade as spread products over benchmark rates, especially Treasuries. When Treasury yields rise, mortgage pricing usually worsens unless MBS spreads tighten enough to offset the move. When Treasury yields fall, mortgage pricing usually improves unless MBS spreads widen. Freddie Mac identifies prevailing interest rates and monetary policy as factors affecting pass-through certificate values.

#### 2. MBS Spread Changes
Mortgage rates do not move solely because Treasuries move. They also move because the **spread** between MBS yields and benchmark rates changes. If MBS spreads widen, mortgage pricing worsens even with no Treasury move. If spreads tighten, mortgage pricing can improve even if Treasury yields are unchanged.

#### 3. Prepayment Expectations
MBS investors care about **prepayment speed** because homeowners can refinance or pay off early. Faster expected prepayments shorten the life of the security when rates fall; slower prepayments extend it when rates rise. This negative convexity is central to mortgage pricing. Freddie Mac explicitly warns that prepayment and yield factors, along with general economic or market conditions, can materially affect MBS pricing and market value.

#### 4. Servicing Value
The lender's economics are affected by the value of mortgage servicing rights or retained servicing cash flow. Since the borrower note rate is split into pass-through rate plus servicing fee plus guaranty fee, servicing assumptions directly affect what retail rates can be profitably priced. Fannie Mae specifies minimum and maximum servicing-fee parameters for MBS mortgage loans and states that total servicing fee is the difference between the loan interest rate and the sum of pass-through rate and guaranty fee.

#### 5. Guaranty Fees
Agency guaranty fees are embedded in execution economics. They reduce the portion of the borrower note rate that can be passed through to investors and therefore affect how note-rate choices map into MBS execution and retail pricing. Fannie Mae's MBS documentation states that pass-through is borrower rate less servicing fee and guaranty fee.

#### 6. Loan-Level Pricing Adjustments (LLPAs) and Credit Overlays
Even if the secondary market improves, the final retail price depends on loan-level adjustments for credit score, LTV, occupancy, product type, cash-out, condo, manufactured housing, and similar risk factors. These factors do not usually determine whether a rate is "available"; they determine the price required for that borrower to obtain it.

#### 7. Lock Period
A 15-day lock, 30-day lock, 45-day lock, and 60-day lock are not the same asset economically. Longer locks generally cost more because they expose the lender to more market risk, hedge cost, and fallout uncertainty. That means the same note rate can be available across lock terms, but pricing deteriorates as lock duration increases.

#### 8. Intraday Volatility and Lender Reprices
Lenders watch live MBS prices and hedge marks throughout the day. If MBS prices deteriorate enough, lenders may issue a negative reprice. If prices improve materially, some lenders issue positive reprices, though often more slowly. Fannie Mae describes live pricing that generally moves with the MBS market, which is the institutional basis for intraday rate-sheet changes.

### How the Pricing Chain Works (Technical Flow)

1. Borrower note rate is selected
2. Lender maps that note rate to an expected pass-through execution
3. Pass-through rate = borrower note rate − servicing fee − guaranty fee
4. Lender evaluates sale price in the secondary market
5. Lender adjusts for hedge cost, pull-through, servicing value, LLPAs, margin, and lock term
6. Retail price is assigned to the borrower as points, par, or lender credit

That is why "rates moved" in retail mortgage language usually means **the pricing attached to the rates moved**. It does not necessarily mean the menu of note rates disappeared. In most cases, the lower rate is still there; it just got more expensive. The higher rate is still there; it may now provide more rebate.

### Why Two Lenders Can Quote the Same Note Rate Differently

Two lenders can quote the same note rate with different pricing because they may have different:

- Hedge performance
- Servicing-retained versus servicing-released strategy
- Gain-on-sale targets
- Delivery channels
- Pull-through assumptions
- Margins
- Operational costs
- Lock desk policies
- Overlays

Fannie Mae's execution materials make clear that lenders operate through different pricing and commitment choices in whole-loan delivery, which supports those quote differences.

### Key Takeaways for Knowledge Base

- Retail note rates are usually available across a broad ladder each day
- The key moving variable is the **price** attached to each rate
- Borrower note rate ≠ MBS pass-through rate. Pass-through = note rate − servicing fee − guaranty fee
- Agency execution structures support quoting many rate options at once
- Pricing changes are driven by Treasury moves, MBS spread moves, prepayment expectations, servicing value, guaranty fees, LLPAs, lock term, and lender margin/hedging decisions
- When markets worsen, the same rate usually remains available but costs more. When markets improve, that same rate may require fewer points or even generate credit
- **The practical rule: Mortgage rates are usually a continuously priced ladder of note-rate options. What changes every day is the market value of each option.**
